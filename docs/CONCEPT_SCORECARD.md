## Why the three additions

**4. Spot the Difference — recommended lead concept.** Watching a play, choosing one of three coloured/numbered/shaped players and seeing a reveal gives newcomers a way in and experts a decision to make. Replay, a scrubber, clues and no response timer reduce barriers. It explicitly features a pass, a puck recovery and a block. Its main weakness is the authored synthetic content. A submission version needs verified match sequences and carefully justified explanations. Predicting the next contribution is not the same as proving the single most important player.

**5. Chain Reaction.** Three tap-to-order event cards reveal the events behind a chance or defensive escape. The answer follows the event chronology rather than subjective player weights. Incorrect sequences can be changed; after two tries a reveal prevents a dead end. Its main risk is becoming too obvious or feeling like a lesson instead of a game. Tonight's test should establish whether people enjoy the act of reconstructing. A real-data version can derive ordering from event timestamps, with editorial review of the story.

**6. Chance or Luck?** Two shots and one choice explain why probability and outcome differ. Diagrams and “out of 100” dots avoid requiring knowledge of xG notation. This has the clearest explicit statistical lesson, but its data score is deliberately lower: current chance values are authored teaching examples, not provider xG or a calibrated model. A final entry should use provider shot-level probabilities with verified meaning and avoid presenting an invented model as Wisehockey output.

## Appeal across ages

Shared interaction principles: large choices, familiar words, no required timer, no compulsory account, no drag-only interactions, optional replay and colour supported by number/shape/text. Animation respects the reduced-motion preference. Broad appeal is an untested hypothesis; age is not a substitute for hockey knowledge or gaming confidence.

Test with both a younger and an older participant, ideally including a newcomer. Observe whether they understand the first action without help, can explain one new thing, finish the round and voluntarily choose another. Do not claim accessibility or age-group validation based on the implementation alone.

## Best route to a stronger submission

1. Choose one flagship game based on observed behaviour tonight; six concepts are an exploration lab, not a polished final product strategy.
2. Replace its synthetic play pack with a permitted, team-private real match workflow. Verify coordinates, clock direction, player identity, event order and narrative against footage.
3. Keep the decisive contribution visible in the reveal. Avoid assigning causal credit from aggregate statistics alone.
4. Document activation: a results-page card, post-period prompt or short social teaser linking into a replayable web experience.
5. Produce the required deck and video, explain the data boundary and show evidence from age-diverse testing.
